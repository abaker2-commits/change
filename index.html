Andrea Rose Baker - Week 5 Assignment 2

From Paper Brief to Secure Tablet

This website analyzes organizational change through the modernization of presidential intelligence delivery, from a legacy paper-based process to secure, tablet-based access. It applies course models from Lewin, Armenakis et al., Tsoukas and Chia, and Weick and Quinn to a real-world, high-stakes change effort involving technology, workflow, leadership, and user adoption.

Review the course models

Case Overview

Why this is organizational change

Organizational change is not only a shift in formal structure. It also includes meaningful changes in routines, technologies, communication patterns, and how work is coordinated. CIA historical material shows that the President’s Daily Brief changed over time in response to presidential habits, delivery needs, format expectations, and technological possibilities, including tablet delivery to the Oval Office by 2015.

This makes PDB modernization a strong case for this assignment because it shows how a trusted legacy process can be redesigned without losing reliability, security, or executive usefulness.

Real-world evidence

Publicly Supported Facts

1961: CIA officials created the President’s Intelligence Checklist to give President Kennedy a shorter, more usable daily intelligence digest.
1964: The product was redesigned for President Johnson and formally became the President’s Daily Brief.
2012 public discussion: The ACLU reported remarks that the president was receiving daily intelligence briefing material on a docked, connectivity-limited iPad.
2015: CIA Director John Brennan publicly stated that the PDB arrived at the Oval Office on an iPad rather than on paper.

Applied Course Models

Lewin

Unfreezing, Moving, Refreezing

  • Unfreezing: stakeholders had to accept that a paper-based briefing model was no longer the only effective way to support modern presidential work habits, speed, and secure mobility.
  • Moving: teams redesigned workflow, device preparation, security controls, briefing format, user support, and delivery procedures around a secure tablet model.
  • Refreezing: the new system became stabilized through repeatable procedures, trust in the new delivery format, and integration into executive routine.

Armenakis et al. (2007)

Readiness for Change

Armenakis and colleagues argue that readiness develops when people believe change is needed, appropriate, achievable, supported by leadership, and beneficial. That logic fits this project closely because a secure-tablet transition could not succeed on technology alone. People had to believe the shift improved mission support rather than simply replacing a familiar system.

DiscrepancyWhy change is necessary.
AppropriatenessWhy this solution fits.
EfficacyWhy people can do it.
SupportWhy leaders back it.
ValenceWhy it matters personally.

Tsoukas and Chia (2002)

Organizational becoming

Tsoukas and Chia argue that organizations are never completely static. People act through routines and categories, but local conditions constantly force interpretation, adjustment, and improvisation. In this project, that means the change was not just “install a device and finish.” It involved continuous reworking of how content, delivery, support, and judgment fit real situations.

Weick and Quinn (1999)

Episodic and continuous change

Weick and Quinn distinguish between episodic change, which is intentional and infrequent, and continuous change, which consists of ongoing small adaptations. The secure-tablet shift can be understood as both: a deliberate launch of a new delivery approach and an extended period of refinement, learning, and adjustment after launch.

Types of change

Type Meaning Application to this case
Episodic change Distinct, intentional intervention that interrupts an established pattern. The introduction of secure tablet delivery for a traditionally paper-centered presidential briefing process.
Continuous change Ongoing cumulative adjustments in daily work and interaction. Refining device handling, content display, workflow timing, user support, and security practices over time.
Planned change Change shaped through design and leadership action rather than waiting for disruption to force reaction. Leaders and specialists aligned technology, mission needs, and executive use requirements before the old model became inadequate.
Reactive risk Change that happens only after failure, misfit, or crisis is too visible to ignore. This project shows why adaptive organizations should not wait until legacy routines become obstacles.

Planned Change Insight

Weick and Quinn note that planned change is often triggered by the failure to build continuously adaptive organizations. In plain terms, if leaders and teams keep learning and adjusting, they may avoid the need for disruptive overhaul later. This case supports that insight because a secure digital delivery model worked best as a strategic adaptation rather than a rushed fix after a visible breakdown.

That lesson applies even beyond government. The strongest organizations treat change readiness as an everyday capability, not an emergency response.

Tsoukas and Chia Analysis

Tsoukas and Chia (2002, p. 573) explain that organized action depends on shared categories, recurring behaviors, and stable understandings about what people do in given situations. Yet those understandings are always somewhat unstable because real situations are never identical. People must interpret rules, extend meanings, and respond to exceptions.

Applied to this project, a person reacting to change is not simply obeying or resisting a new rule. That person is trying to decide whether old habits still fit the situation, whether the new process makes sense, and how to act competently when the environment changes. This helps explain why reactions to change are often cautious at first: people are protecting reliability while testing a new pattern of action.

Changing Behaviors Important future workplace attitudes and skills include adaptability, disciplined communication, systems thinking, learning agility, emotional steadiness, and respect for security and process integrity.
Global vs Local A global organization usually faces greater pressure to address continuous change because it operates across more complex political, technical, cultural, and risk environments than a local organization does.
Leadership Lesson Effective leaders do not just announce change. They make it understandable, legitimate, supported, and doable for the people who must carry it out every day.
