<div class="container grid-2">
<article class="panel">
<p class="kicker">Case overview</p>
<h2>Why this is organizational change</h2>
<p>Organizational change is not only a shift in structure. It also includes meaningful changes in routines, technologies, communication patterns, and how work is coordinated. CIA historical material shows that the President’s Daily Brief changed over time in response to presidential habits, delivery needs, format expectations, and technological possibilities, culminating in tablet delivery to the Oval Office by 2015.</p>
<p>This makes the PDB modernization a strong case for this assignment because it shows how a trusted legacy process can be redesigned without losing reliability, security, or executive usefulness.</p>
</article>
<article class="panel">
<p class="kicker">Real-world evidence</p>
<h2>Publicly supported facts</h2>
<div class="timeline">
<div class="timeline-item"><strong>1961:</strong> CIA officials created the President’s Intelligence Checklist to give President Kennedy a shorter, more usable daily intelligence digest.</div>
<div class="timeline-item"><strong>1964:</strong> The product was redesigned for President Johnson and formally became the President’s Daily Brief.</div>
<div class="timeline-item"><strong>2012 public discussion:</strong> ACLU reported remarks that the president was receiving daily intelligence briefing material on a docked, connectivity-limited iPad.</div>
<div class="timeline-item"><strong>2015:</strong> CIA Director John Brennan publicly stated that the PDB arrived at the Oval Office on an iPad instead of paper.</div>
</div>
</article>
</div>
</section>

<section id="models">
<div class="container">
<h2>Course models applied to the project</h2>
<div class="grid-2">
<article class="model">
<p class="kicker">Lewin</p>
<h3>Unfreezing, moving, refreezing</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Unfreezing:</strong> stakeholders had to accept that a paper-based briefing model was no longer the only effective way to support modern presidential work habits, speed, and secure mobility.</li>
<li><strong>Moving:</strong> teams redesigned workflow, device preparation, security controls, briefing format, user support, and delivery procedures around a secure tablet model.</li>
<li><strong>Refreezing:</strong> the new system became stabilized through repeatable procedures, trust in the new delivery format, and integration into executive routine.</li>
</ul>
</article>
<article class="model">
<p class="kicker">Armenakis et al. (2007)</p>
<h3>Readiness for change</h3>
<p>Armenakis and colleagues argue that readiness develops when people believe change is needed, appropriate, achievable, supported by leadership, and beneficial. That logic fits this project closely because a secure-tablet transition could not succeed on technology alone. People had to believe the shift improved mission support rather than simply replacing a familiar system.</p>
<div class="process" aria-label="Readiness process">
<div class="step"><span>Discrepancy</span>Why change is necessary.</div>
<div class="step"><span>Appropriateness</span>Why this solution fits.</div>
<div class="step"><span>Efficacy</span>Why people can do it.</div>
<div class="step"><span>Support</span>Why leaders back it.</div>
<div class="step"><span>Valence</span>Why it matters personally.</div>
</div>
</article>
<article class="model">
<p class="kicker">Tsoukas and Chia (2002)</p>
<h3>Organizational becoming</h3>
<p>Tsoukas and Chia argue that organizations are never completely static. People act through routines and categories, but local conditions constantly force interpretation, adjustment, and improvisation. In this project, that means the change was not just “install a device and finish.” It involved continuous reworking of how content, delivery, support, and judgment fit real situations.</p>
</article>
<article class="model">
<p class="kicker">Weick and Quinn (1999)</p>
<h3>Episodic and continuous change</h3>
<p>Weick and Quinn distinguish between episodic change, which is intentional and infrequent, and continuous change, which consists of ongoing small adaptations. The secure-tablet shift can be understood as both: a deliberate launch of a new delivery approach and an extended period of refinement, learning, and adjustment after launch.</p>
</article>
</div>
</div>
</section>

<section id="change">
<div class="container grid-2">
<article class="panel comparison">
<h2>Types of change</h2>
<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Type</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Application to this case</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Episodic change</td><td>Distinct, intentional intervention that interrupts an established pattern.</td><td>The introduction of secure tablet delivery for a traditionally paper-centered presidential briefing process.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Continuous change</td><td>Ongoing cumulative adjustments in daily work and interaction.</td><td>Refining device handling, content display, workflow timing, user support, and security practices over time.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Planned change</td><td>Change shaped through design and leadership action rather than waiting for disruption to force reaction.</td><td>Leaders and specialists aligned technology, mission needs, and executive use requirements before the old model became inadequate.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Reactive risk</td><td>Change that happens only after failure, misfit, or crisis is too visible to ignore.</td><td>This project shows why adaptive organizations should not wait until legacy routines become obstacles.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</article>
<article class="panel">
<h2>Planned change insight</h2>
<p>Weick and Quinn note that planned change is often triggered by the failure to build continuously adaptive organizations. In plain terms, if leaders and teams keep learning and adjusting, they may avoid the need for disruptive overhaul later. This case supports that insight because a secure digital delivery model worked best as a strategic adaptation rather than a rushed fix after a visible breakdown.</p>
<p>That lesson applies even beyond government. The strongest organizations treat change readiness as an everyday capability, not an emergency response.</p>
</article>
</div>
</section>

<section id="analysis">
<div class="container grid-2">
<article class="panel">
<h2>Tsoukas and Chia page 573 analysis</h2>
<p>On page 573, Tsoukas and Chia explain that organized action depends on shared categories, recurring behaviors, and stable understandings about what people do in given situations. Yet those understandings are always somewhat unstable because real situations are never identical. People must interpret rules, extend meanings, and respond to exceptions.</p>
<p>Applied to this project, a person reacting to change is not simply obeying or resisting a new rule. That person is trying to decide whether old habits still fit the situation, whether the new process makes sense, and how to act competently when the environment changes. This explains why change reactions are often cautious at first: people are protecting reliability while they test a new pattern of action.</p>
</article>
